Branches of the Relationship with the Quran

The Quran and Psychology: Which Does More to Change a Person’s Thought and Conduct?

  • 📅 July 5, 2026
  • ⏱️ 18 min read
  • 📖 … completed reads
  • ❤️ …

These are reflections and passing thoughts in which I set the Noble Quran’s way of changing a person beside psychology’s: in conduct, in belief, and in thought.

And where did they come from? From experience: some of it I witnessed, some I heard of, and some I lived myself on my journey of memorizing the Noble Quran, a journey of a year and nine months, by the grace of Allah the Exalted.

I did not draw them from particular books or sources. I believe the truest knowledge is the knowledge that comes from the sweetness and the bitterness of experience. Books and studies can carry a surplus of philosophy that no one needs, like a tree so overgrown with branches and boughs, twigs and tendrils, that it begs to be pruned, and a person can barely find in it the fruit he came looking for.

Lived experience, though, is the truer teacher, and brings the truth closer.

And when I hold this up against psychology, I recall what Dr. Abdullah Al-Jaber said in an episode titled, I believe, “How Can We Be Happy in Our Relationships”: the trouble with psychology is that it has penned the human being into a very narrow space, the space of feelings and emotions.

It has located all of a person’s ailments in the emotions alone: anxiety, depression, panic. Do you know of anything else? Has anyone ever told you there are other psychological ailments? Treatment and disorder alike are confined to feelings and emotions, as though a human being had no other troubles. Yet the human world of troubles is vast: troubles of the tongue, troubles of language, cultural troubles, social troubles, and much else besides, and troubles of the spirit and of faith.

Dr. Abdullah Al-Jaber was right on these points when he said that the approach of Islam (or, to return to his own words, the approach of the Quran) is that a person’s ailments lie in the heart. In its way of changing people, the Noble Quran fixes its gaze on the diseases of the heart and sets them squarely before the eyes of whoever reads it and keeps its company. It speaks to us of the heart’s diseases, of doubt and hypocrisy, of vanity and showing off: (And [also] those who spend of their wealth to be seen by the people), and so much more.

This does not mean psychology is wrong in everything. It has sound theories, and after Allah I owe it a debt: it helped me correct and change many ways of thinking and habits of mind, and more besides. I do not deny it; it is a respected discipline in its own right. But it has certain problems, and it is those certain problems we are speaking of.

The Noble Quran works on moral healing, both in thought and in conduct. And seeking healing and treatment through the Noble Quran works differently from the psychological way: it rests on repetition, on a constant daily portion (wird) that a person never lets go of, and on deepening the relationship and letting it branch out: the “branches of the relationship with the Quran,” as I have named them.

That is, a person will not draw healing and guidance from the Noble Quran unless he multiplies the branches of his bond with it. He must not settle for a portion of recitation alone, or for listening alone, but add to them understanding the Quran and its exegesis, reading about it, reflecting on it, and acting on it.

And when a psychologist comes along and says, “Sometimes a man comes to me who has memorized the Noble Quran, an imam and preacher at a mosque, and still he has psychological problems!”, we call that psychologist to account and tell him: you should not say such things and spread them among people. Why? Because this is an individual problem, his problem alone; he may have misunderstood the Quran. Let us take an example:

An educator, Dr. Mustafa Abu Saad, related in one of his episodes that a preacher and mosque imam came to his clinic with a psychological problem (no need to name it): a certain ugly addiction. So what was the cause? How can a man like that guard his prayers and still fall into such a thing? Stories like this unsettle the listener, who says to himself: so even religion won’t help, even the Quran won’t help, so why waste my time? That is why the case must be explained.

He related that this man had used a legal stratagem (ḥīlah fiqhiyyah) to justify the wrong to himself. He said: “I know the Sharia, so I went searching until I found a fatwa that allowed me this thing, just as my desires and my mood wanted.” So the problem he fell into, the disease in his soul: he himself is its cause. Not the Quran, and not religion.

The problem is his. Allah the Exalted says in Surat al-Baqarah: (In their hearts is disease). The scholars said this shows that the disease comes from the servant himself: (In their hearts); that is, the disease springs from the servant’s own soul, not from religion and not from the Quran, so do not lay it at the door of the Sharia. He has erred in his approach to the Quran, erred in his understanding of prayer, erred in his understanding of the Sharia. So why not explain this case to people?

Why the sweeping statement? Why cite it and say, “I know many religious people, even a mosque preacher, who have addictions and problems”? Why not show us the error this man fell into, instead of tying things together in a way that clouds people’s understanding and plants doubt about their religion? I know this is not what the psychologist intends when he says it, but as long as it remains a sweeping statement, there is no call for it.

This man erred in his way of understanding the Quran: he used a legal stratagem. Some scholars see no harm in using legal stratagems within their proper scope, to lift hardship and the like; but they are not to be used to slip free of what is forbidden, of the prescribed limits, and so on.

So we come back to the question once more: when someone claims that the Noble Quran has not healed his problems and ailments, we say: that is because something is wrong in how he deals with the Quran and in his approach to it, and the problem may well be that he does not act on it. Allah the Exalted says in Surat al-Ma’idah: (Say, “O People of the Scripture, you are [standing] on nothing until you uphold [the law of] the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to you from your Lord.”), and that is the Quran.

That is: you stand on nothing until you act on the Quran and live it out. It is not about memorizing the Quran so that when we ask you, “How many verses?” you have the answer ready (mā shā’ Allāh), or about memorizing it in all ten readings. There is something vital beyond that: that he upholds it. Allah the Exalted says: (And establish prayer). What does that mean? That he answers the call of prayer and keeps its conditions, its pillars, its obligations, its humility, and so on; that is, that he acts.

If he does not act on the Quran, he is bound to run into problems; the ailments will linger in his soul, and the Quran will be of no benefit to him. Yes, for the Quran is either for you or against you.

That is why one of the things that once held me back from memorizing the Quran was the poor examples I saw among those who had memorized it. Because they did not live by the Quran, I saw in them ugly, negative behavior and crooked character, and I would say: why should I memorize the Quran only to end up like them?

To be frank, they set me a poor example. But my thinking changed after that, as I came to understand His words, the Exalted: (O you who have believed, upon you is [responsibility for] yourselves. Those who have gone astray will not harm you when you have been guided). That verse was my answer. If someone suffers from hypocrisy, or psychological ailments, or troubles of faith and spirit, that is because of him, not because of the Quran; he did not live it and did not act on it.

Or the cause may be that he fell short in understanding the Quran. It is not as if a person simply reads the Quran and that is the end of it. You must keep a daily portion of the Quran, and that is a fine and beautiful thing; but for healing these problems and ailments, one must also be reminded to let the relationship branch out. To branch out the relationship means to bind yourself to the Quran in many ways: at times through understanding and exegesis, at times through reflection and contemplation, at times through listening and seeking healing. This is how seeking healing, and finding it, through the Noble Quran comes true.

But to read without living it: then naturally he will fall into psychological troubles. And to read without understanding the Quran, without knowing what it says: then his healing through the Quran will be incomplete.

Now we come to the Quran’s way of changing a person. It rests, as we said, on multiplying the branches of the relationship with the Quran, reflection and action among them, and on varying them, rather than keeping to only one kind of bond with the Quran.

The second point: the Noble Quran brings about change through repetition. Memorizing the Quran depends on endless repetition; and after memorizing, the one who has memorized it keeps a set portion of the Quran, and that too is repetition. No sooner does he finish one portion, one complete reading, than he begins another; so the wisdom, the knowledge, and the healing wash over him again and again. This repetition plays its part in changing a person, and the power of repetition to change people is well known in media and in psychology.

The third point: the way of long companionship, of sitting together and growing close. Whoever goes to a psychologist today sits with him for an hour or two, a session or two or three, and I doubt it is more than an hour or two, over a short, limited span. That is why a person may relapse (in psychology’s sense of the word): his bad habits come back, and he needs the clinic again. For the change psychology brings to the human soul is a change of the branches, not of the root; not fundamental, not essential, not radical. It changes the surface and leaves the depths untouched.

That is why a person may spend perhaps eighteen years in psychology and its sciences, reading books and more, and nothing in him changes; in truth, his problems and his frustrations only grow.

So tell me, then: why does the Quran turn a person a hundred and eighty degrees in a single year? Read about it, listen to the experiences and the stories of those who have memorized the Quran, and how the Quran changed them until they became someone other than who they had been; ([It will be] on the Day the earth will be replaced by another earth, and the heavens [as well]); and so too the human soul is replaced after the Quran: the soul is no longer the same soul, nor the spirit the same spirit. It is as if the Quran takes our old nervous system, throws it away, and hands us a new one. That is how I see it: it gives us new blood, a new spirit, a new life.

So do not be surprised when you hear many who have memorized the Quran say: today I was born; today is my true birthday.

And His words, the Exalted: (Say, “If Allah had willed, I would not have recited it to you, nor would He have made it known to you, for I had remained among you a lifetime before it. Then will you not reason?”). What I take from it (and this is my own reflection) is as if he were saying: my life and my years before the revelation came to me were one thing, and my life and my years after the revelation are something else entirely; after the Quran I was given a new birthday. (for I had remained among you a lifetime before it): I take from the verse that there is a lifetime after it, as though it were a new life.

In any case, as we were saying: whoever goes to a psychologist sits with him for an hour, or two, or three, and so his change is no true change. But whoever memorizes the Noble Quran sits with the Quran five or six hours a day. Many who have memorized it say: we slept only four or five hours a day, and all the rest of the time we were reciting and repeating.

And once he has memorized the Quran, he gives it his time so as not to forget it; otherwise, what was the point of the year or two he spent memorizing it? So the reciter and companion of the Quran, through long hours in its company, takes on the attributes and the names of the Quran.

Among the names of the Quran: al-Karīm (the Noble), al-Ḥakīm (the Wise), and al-Muḥkam (the Firmly Set). And al-Muḥkam (the word just came to me), together with ḥukm (judgment) and ḥikmah (wisdom), all carry the sense that it holds a person in check: it governs his will, his desires, his whims, his thoughts, and his beliefs.

For the Noble Quran disciplines a person in all these things: it is muḥkam, firmly set, and it makes a person firmly set. It is human nature for thoughts to come: let’s do this, and the Quran checks him, and he says: no, I don’t want to do that, I don’t want to say that. It makes a person firm, and the Quran shapes its reader with lofty qualities: the Quran is noble and makes its companion noble; the Quran is wise and makes its companion wise, so that you find in him marvelous reflections and beautiful insights into the Noble Quran.

And the Quran is called al-ʿAẓīm, the Great, and its companion becomes great. In the sense of the authentic hadith, one of the Companions said: whoever among us had read al-Baqarah, that is, memorized it, rose high in our esteem; that is, he becomes a great person, and his greatness shows in how people take him to their hearts, and more.

Another of the Quran’s names is the Rope: (And hold firmly to the rope of Allah), and the rope of Allah is the Quran. And in the sense of another authentic hadith: the Quran is the rope of Allah stretched from the sky to the earth, one end in the Hand of Allah, the other in the hand of the servant who holds fast to it.

This image is always before my mind’s eye: I picture ropes let down from the sky to the earth, and those who have memorized the Quran clinging to them, while others hold no rope at all. I picture the whole globe, and people holding on to these ropes: these are the saved, by Allah’s leave, if Allah wills, for this lies in the Hand of Allah the Exalted. But these, the Quran saves from their cravings, saves from the adornment of this worldly life, its glitter, and its delusions.

As Dr. Ahmed Abdel Moneim said: the more a person reads the Noble Quran, the more it loosens his hold on this world and kindles his longing for the Hereafter. And now I recall the verses of Surat al-Baqarah: (And who believe in what has been revealed to you, [O Muhammad], and what was revealed before you, and of the Hereafter they are certain [in faith]).

Here restriction (ḥaṣr) is at work: certainty is confined to the Hereafter alone, as though certainty about anything else hardly counts. A person may be certain about this world, yet He confined certainty and reserved it for the Hereafter.

Among the names of the Quran: the Light, and it passes its light on to its companion. And this light is a true, distinguishing light; that is, it wells up from the very core of the person. The hypocrite may benefit from the Quran too, but the benefit is slight and the light is slight, and whatever light the hypocrite has is an outer light, worn on the outside.

The believer’s light, though, is an inner light, from within, because of the true and original light he carries in his heart. (Their example is that of one who kindled a fire, but when it illuminated what was around him, Allah took away their light): here Allah the Exalted describes the hypocrites as having light (nūr), and He did not say “their radiance” (ḍiyāʾihim), for the hypocrite has no radiance like the believer’s, only light (and light, as you know, is fainter and less than radiance). And (it illuminated what was around him) shows that the hypocrite’s light is an outer light, its source outside him. Further proof is the word istawqada, “kindled”: that is, he sought another’s help to kindle this fire.

Had this light come from his own essence and core, He would have said awqada, “lit”; but He said istawqada. So the believer’s light is original and true: the Quran lights up his face, his heart, his spirit, and his tongue.

And here too the limits of psychology as a means of change come into view: it changes only feelings and the lightest of things.

The Noble Quran’s change, however, reaches everything: it changes even the tongue, even the language; even a person’s eloquence is transformed, even his memory. You hear those who memorize the Quran say: our memory has grown strong. And do not even ask about this, for those who have memorized the Quran do not speak of it; they live, truly, in bliss, but some of them keep that bliss to themselves, guarding it, afraid for it.

And I say: every pleasure short of the Quran is incomplete. When a person memorizes the Quran, reflects on it, and truly reaches it, his pleasure is complete, even if the pleasures around him fall short, even if the world around him falls short.

Sheikh Abdulsalam Al-Shuwaier related in one of his lectures that a non-Arab Muslim had memorized the Quran and never slipped in its grammar; the Sheikh said: I did not find a single mistake in him. Why? Because the Noble Quran had passed its eloquence on to him. Among the Quran’s names are al-Bayān (the Clear Statement) and al-Mubīn (the Clear): (These are the verses of the clear Book); and so it passes on to its companion clarity, eloquence, and language, and it straightens and heals a person’s troubles of tongue and speech.

Does psychology do this? Psychology treats a limited range of things. And we do not belittle the worth of psychology; these are reflections, not idle talk. It is a discipline in its own right that people study for years, and no one should come along and stamp it “wrong”! Nor do we downplay its importance; it matters, and its theories matter. But we are pointing out certain problems in it: it confines the human being to the psyche alone, then confines the psyche to emotions and feelings, while the human being, as a whole and in every part, is far greater than that.

A person has a bond with his Lord, and in that bond there are problems he must resolve, and the Quran helps him resolve them. He has troubles in his bond and his dialogue with himself, and troubles in his bond with others and his dealings with them; and the Noble Quran heals all of these.

And then there is the question of the source, as Dr. Ahmed Abdel Moneim observed (and we salute him for it): the schools of psychology, Freud’s psychoanalysis, then behaviorism, and others, have their problems and their errors, because they are made by human beings. But Allah, glorified and exalted is He, is the One who created human beings and the human soul, and He knows best what sets it right and what flaws it holds: (Does He who created not know, while He is the Subtle, the Acquainted?). Allah the Exalted knows this soul best, and what sets it right, and what heals it; and that is why the Noble Quran is the soundest and finest of all ways to straighten and reshape the human soul.

— Shoroog Almehmadi
